# Literature and reference log

All 20 seed entries from PRD.md Section 14.4 were checked against web search results (title,
authors, venue, year) and all 20 verified as real publications or a real dataset. The table below
keeps the 15 most directly relevant to telltale, per PRD's instruction to keep 12 to 15 verified
entries in the final log. Five verified entries were set aside only for space and topical overlap,
not because anything about them was wrong: see "Verified but not included" at the bottom.

| Gama, Zliobaite, Bifet, Pechenizkiy, Bouchachia. A survey on concept drift adaptation. ACM Computing Surveys, 46(4), article 44, 2014. | How to characterize and adapt to concept drift in online supervised learning. | Survey: categorizes adaptive learning strategies and evaluation methodology across the field. | Establishes the vocabulary telltale relies on (sudden, gradual, recurring, pure concept drift) and confirms these are the standard categories researchers evaluate against. | A survey, not a method: gives no single detector to adopt directly. | Chose the S1/S2/S3/S4 scenario taxonomy (sudden, gradual, recurring, concept) to match the field's standard categories rather than inventing a bespoke one. |
| Webb, Hyde, Cao, Nguyen, Petitjean. Characterizing concept drift. Data Mining and Knowledge Discovery, 30(4), 964-994, 2016. | Prior drift categorizations were qualitative; the field lacked formal, quantitative definitions. | Proposes formal definitions and a taxonomy of drift types based on how the joint distribution P(X,y) changes. | Gives a precise, defensible distinction between a change in P(X) (covariate drift) and a change in P(y given X) (concept drift), which is exactly the line telltale's scenarios are built to test. | Definitional and taxonomic, not an evaluation method for a specific detector. | Justifies treating S1 to S3 (input marginal changes) and S4 (label-generating function changes, marginals fixed) as genuinely different drift types rather than variations of one thing. |
| Dal Pozzolo, Caelen, Le Borgne, Waterschoot, Bontempi. Learned lessons in credit card fraud detection from a practitioner perspective. Expert Systems with Applications, 41(10), 4915-4928, 2014. | What actually goes wrong when deploying fraud classifiers in production. | Practitioner-perspective analysis of a real card-fraud pipeline. | Names class imbalance, concept drift and limited, delayed feedback as the practical obstacles, the same three telltale is built around. | Specific to one processor's pipeline and card data; generalization to other fraud domains is not established. | Grounded the choice of a 2 percent synthetic base fraud rate and the delayed-label framing in a real deployment's reported obstacles, not just an abstract assumption. |
| Dal Pozzolo, Boracchi, Caelen, Alippi, Bontempi. Credit card fraud detection and concept-drift adaptation with delayed supervised information. IJCNN, 1-8, 2015. | Fraud-detection systems only receive a small set of delayed, investigator-confirmed labels, not the true label for every transaction. | Compares pooling delayed labels with alert feedback against training two separate classifiers and aggregating their outputs. | Handling feedback labels separately from delayed verified labels improves alert precision, i.e. the two label sources are not interchangeable. | Only two specific aggregation strategies are compared; does not address label-free monitoring at all. | Directly supports telltale's label vault design, which treats "matured" labels as a distinct, delayed resource rather than assuming all labels arrive uniformly. |
| Dal Pozzolo, Boracchi, Caelen, Alippi, Bontempi. Credit card fraud detection: a realistic modeling and a novel learning strategy. IEEE Transactions on Neural Networks and Learning Systems, 29(8), 3784-3797, 2018. | Existing fraud-detection evaluations do not realistically model verification latency, class imbalance and concept drift together. | Formalizes the operating conditions of a real fraud-detection stream and proposes a learning strategy addressing all three jointly. | "Verification latency" is this paper's term for exactly telltale's label delay D; treating it jointly with concept drift and imbalance, rather than one at a time, is the paper's central argument. | A single learning strategy evaluated on one processor's data, not a general-purpose monitoring framework. | Reinforced the decision to model label delay as a first-class simulation parameter (D) rather than an afterthought, and to report metrics that remain meaningful under class imbalance (PRD Section 8's "never report accuracy" rule). |
| Rabanser, Guennemann, Lipton. Failing loudly: an empirical study of methods for detecting dataset shift. NeurIPS, 1396-1408, 2019. | Many dataset-shift detectors exist; which actually work, and how do you explain what shifted. | Empirical comparison of two-sample tests (including dimensionality-reduced ones) for shift detection, plus methods for characterizing which features drove a detected shift. | Two-sample testing with a dimensionality-reduction step performed best across the shifts tested; domain-discriminating approaches help explain a shift rather than just flag it. | Benchmarked on image and tabular shift benchmarks, not a fraud or delayed-label setting. | Supported the decision to log which features drove an input-drift alarm (PRD 6.3), not just the alarm itself, since explaining a shift is treated as a first-class requirement by this literature, not a nice-to-have. |
| Bifet, Gavalda. Learning from time-changing data with adaptive windowing. SIAM International Conference on Data Mining, 443-448, 2007. | Fixed-size sliding windows force a hand-picked time scale for detecting change. | ADWIN: a sliding window whose size adapts automatically, shrinking when change is detected and growing when the stream is stable, with proven bounds on false positive/negative rates. | A classical, well-understood alternative to telltale's fixed-window CUSUM approach; defines the "adaptive window" branch of the field. | Operates on a single scalar statistic at a time and needs a labeled error signal in its most common use, so it does not directly solve label-free monitoring. | Used as a comparison point in the desk-study table (report Section 8) for why telltale uses a fixed window with a separate CUSUM rather than an adaptive window. |
| Gama, Medas, Castillo, Rodrigues. Learning with drift detection. SBIA (Advances in Artificial Intelligence), 2004. | Detecting when a classifier's error rate has genuinely changed, not just fluctuated. | DDM: tracks the online error rate against a warning level and a drift level, retraining once the drift level is crossed. | The most widely used classical drift detector; establishes the "retrain once a threshold is crossed" pattern telltale's CUSUM-based policy generalizes. | Needs a labeled error signal at every step, which is exactly what telltale's delayed labels make unavailable until D windows later. | Directly motivated the backstop (PRD 6.6): DDM's error-rate-triggered retrain idea is preserved, but only applied once labels mature, as a slower backstop behind the label-free monitor. |
| Page. Continuous inspection schemes. Biometrika, 41, 100-115, 1954. | Detecting a persistent shift in a process's mean from a stream of noisy observations, without over-reacting to single outliers. | CUSUM: accumulate the excess of each observation over a reference value, signal when the accumulated sum crosses a threshold; introduces the average run length (ARL) as the metric for how often false alarms occur. | This is the exact mechanism telltale's evidence combiner implements (PRD 6.5) and the ARL concept telltale's analytical model (A2) uses to predict the false alarm rate. | A classical statistical process control tool, not designed for multivariate, model-derived evidence signals. | Directly the basis of `telltale/monitors/cusum.py`: the slack-and-height one-sided accumulation rule is Page's original formulation applied to a drift-evidence signal instead of a process measurement. |
| Garg, Balakrishnan, Lipton, Neyshabur, Sedghi. Leveraging unlabeled data to predict out-of-distribution performance. ICLR, 2022. | Predicting how much a model's accuracy will degrade under distribution shift using only unlabeled target data. | ATC (Average Thresholded Confidence): learns a confidence threshold on labeled source data, then predicts target performance from the share of unlabeled target examples above that threshold. | The closest prior art to telltale's core premise: estimating performance degradation from unlabeled signals alone (confidence-based), without any new labels. | Evaluated on image classification benchmarks (ImageNet, CIFAR) under synthetic and natural shift, not a streaming, delayed-label, fraud setting. | Supports the design decision to include predictive-entropy and alert-rate-ratio as score-based evidence signals (PRD 6.4): ATC shows confidence-derived statistics alone carry real information about performance degradation. |
| Gretton, Borgwardt, Rasch, Schoelkopf, Smola. A kernel two-sample test. Journal of Machine Learning Research, 13, 723-773, 2012. | Testing whether two samples come from the same distribution without assuming a parametric form. | Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD): the largest difference in expectations over functions in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space, with distribution-free hypothesis tests built on it. | A classifier-free, nonparametric two-sample test; a natural alternative to telltale's PSI/KS-based input drift monitor. | Kernel choice and bandwidth matter a great deal in practice and are not fixed automatically; computationally heavier than PSI at telltale's window sizes. | Used as a comparison point in the desk-study table: PSI and KS were chosen over MMD for telltale's input monitor mainly for computational cost at a 2000-row window size and interpretability (per-feature PSI explains an alarm; an MMD statistic does not, on its own). |
| Lipton, Wang, Smola. Detecting and correcting for label shift with black box predictors. ICML, 2018. | Correcting for a change in the label marginal P(y) while P(X given y) stays fixed, using only a black-box classifier's predictions. | BBSE (Black Box Shift Estimation): estimates the test label distribution from a classifier's confusion matrix on source data and its predictions on target data. | A contrasting failure mode to telltale's main scenarios: label shift (prevalence changes, relationship stable) rather than covariate or concept drift; directly relevant to the P2 scenario S7 (prevalence shift). | Needs an invertible confusion matrix and, like most label-shift correction, is not built for a streaming, delayed-label setting. | Clarified why S7 (fraud prevalence doubling) is conceptually distinct from S1 to S5 and should be treated as its own scenario rather than folded into covariate drift. |
| Shimodaira. Improving predictive inference under covariate shift by weighting the log-likelihood function. Journal of Statistical Planning and Inference, 90, 227-244, 2000. | Standard maximum likelihood is not optimal when training and test inputs come from different distributions but the conditional P(y given X) is unchanged. | Importance-weighted log-likelihood: reweight training examples by the ratio of test to train input density. | Introduced the term "covariate shift" and the importance-weighting idea that underlies interim adaptation. | The importance ratio itself must be estimated, which is hard in high dimensions and adds its own variance. | Basis for the P1 interim-adaptation ablation (PRD 6.8): reweighting matured labels by an estimated density ratio between reference and recent windows, to bridge the gap until the next full retrain. |
| Siddiqi. Credit Risk Scorecards: Developing and Implementing Intelligent Credit Scoring. Wiley, 2006. | How practicing credit-risk teams monitor whether a scorecard's population has drifted from when it was built. | Population Stability Index (PSI): a binned, asymmetric discrepancy measure between two distributions, with PSI below 0.1 read as stable and above 0.25 as a population shift in industry practice. | This is the exact statistic telltale's input and score monitors are built on (PRD 6.3, 6.4), including the practitioner thresholds that motivated the generator's own 0.25 PSI check for "near null" in the Phase 1 acceptance tests. | An industry handbook, not a peer-reviewed statistical paper; PSI's behavior under small samples and sensitivity to binning (PRD risk 9) is not formally analyzed here. | Directly the source of PSI as telltale's primary drift statistic, and of the 0.25 threshold used informally while checking that S4 leaves feature marginals near-null. |

Credit Card Fraud Detection (Kaggle dataset, mlg-ulb/creditcardfraud). | Public benchmark for card-fraud detection research. | 284,807 European cardholder transactions from September 2013, 492 of them fraud, 28 PCA-anonymised features plus Time and Amount. | The dataset PRD Section 5.4's P1 real-data check is built on; the exact transaction and fraud counts were confirmed. | Only 492 frauds over two days, anonymised PCA features (no semantic meaning, so no feature-level alarm explanation is possible), and no natural drift to exploit, hence telltale's semi-synthetic approach of injecting drift into it. | Confirms the P1 real-data check's stated limitation (PRD 5.4) that detection-only evaluation, not retraining, is appropriate given how little natural variation two days of data contain. |

## Review, grouped by theme

Three themes ground telltale's method. The first is concept drift taxonomy and detection:
Gama et al. (2014) and Webb et al. (2016) give the vocabulary and formal distinction between a
changing input distribution and a changing input-output relationship that telltale's scenario
design (S1 to S3 versus S4) is built on, while Bifet and Gavalda (2007) and Gama et al. (2004)
are the classical adaptive-window and error-rate detectors that motivate why telltale needs
something different: both need a labeled error signal at every step, which delayed labels make
unavailable. The second is fraud detection under delayed labels specifically: the three Dal
Pozzolo et al. papers (2014, 2015, 2018) are the closest domain-specific prior work, naming
verification latency, imbalance and drift as the practical obstacles and showing that pooling
versus separating delayed and feedback labels changes results, which is exactly the distinction
telltale's label vault and backstop are built to respect. The third is label-free and
unsupervised signals for detecting degradation: Rabanser et al. (2019) and Gretton et al. (2012)
cover two-sample testing as a general tool, Siddiqi (2006) supplies PSI as the specific statistic
telltale uses, and Garg et al. (2022) is the closest prior work to telltale's central claim, that
unlabeled, confidence-derived signals carry real information about performance degradation.
Shimodaira (2000) and Lipton et al. (2018) round this out by giving the theoretical vocabulary
(covariate shift, label shift) for drift types telltale treats as distinct scenarios, and Page
(1954) supplies the CUSUM mechanism that turns any of these per-window statistics into a
persistence-aware alarm. The Kaggle dataset is the one real-world artifact tying the synthetic
scenarios back to an actual fraud stream, within the limits its size and anonymisation allow.

## The gap telltale fills

None of the sources above combine three things at once: a detector that works without labels
(the Dal Pozzolo and DDM-style work needs a labeled error signal; Garg et al. gets close but is
not evaluated under label delay or on a streaming retrain policy), explicit treatment of the
delay between a transaction and its confirmed label as a first-class simulation parameter rather
than an assumption, and a retrain policy that is allowed to defer based on a label-free alarm
while a separate, slower backstop (DDM's error-rate idea, applied once labels mature) catches
what the label-free signals structurally cannot see (pure concept drift, S4). That combination,
label-free early warning plus a delayed-label backstop plus a deferred retrain timed to when
enough fresh labels exist, is the gap this project fills.

## Verified but not included in the final table

These five seed entries were confirmed as real publications during the same search pass but left
out of the 15-entry table above for space and topical overlap with an entry already included, not
because of any doubt about them:

- Lu, Liu, Dong, Gu, Gama, Zhang. Learning under concept drift: a review. IEEE TKDE, 31(12),
  2346-2363, 2019. Confirmed (over 130 publications reviewed, three-part framework of detection,
  understanding, adaptation). Overlaps with Gama et al. (2014) already in the table.
- Hendrycks, Gimpel. A baseline for detecting misclassified and out-of-distribution examples in
  neural networks. ICLR, 2017. Confirmed (softmax-probability baseline for OOD detection).
  Overlaps with Garg et al. (2022), which is a more direct match to telltale's confidence-based
  score signals.
- Sugiyama, Krauledat, Mueller. Covariate shift adaptation by importance weighted cross
  validation. JMLR, 8, 985-1005, 2007. Confirmed (unbiased importance-weighted cross-validation
  under covariate shift). Overlaps with Shimodaira (2000), the earlier and more foundational
  source for the same P1 interim-adaptation idea.
- Sculley and colleagues. Hidden technical debt in machine learning systems. NeurIPS,
  2503-2511, 2015. Confirmed (names hidden feedback loops, entanglement, and undeclared
  consumers as ML-specific maintenance risks). Relevant to the project's own risk list (PRD 14.3
  item 5, the feedback-loop risk) rather than to the method itself.
- Ovadia, Fertig, Ren, Nado, Sculley, Nowozin, Dillon, Lakshminarayanan, Snoek. Can you trust
  your model's uncertainty? Evaluating predictive uncertainty under dataset shift. NeurIPS, 2019.
  Confirmed (large-scale benchmark of uncertainty methods under dataset shift). General
  background for using predictive entropy as a drift signal, without a specific design choice
  traceable to it the way Garg et al. (2022) has one.
